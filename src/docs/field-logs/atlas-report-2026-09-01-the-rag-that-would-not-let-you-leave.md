## The Object

The Maple Leaf Rag Zone is a passive slick at `/maple-leaf-rag-zone/`, a sibling to ZOOT. It cross-fades Shadow Zone ephemera under a warm maple palette while large red leaf glyphs drift downward and scatter away from the pointer. Behind an entry gate — "Extra Crispy" for sound, "Silent Running" for none — a single local file loops: `mapleleafrag.mp3`. There is no roaming text and no interactive field; the zone is meant to be watched and heard, not read. All of this is verified directly from the repository.

What the zone does not carry, until now, is any record of where that loop came from.

## The Rag Itself

"Maple Leaf Rag" is Scott Joplin's, published in Sedalia, Missouri in 1899 under a contract with the publisher John Stillwell Stark — signed that August, for a royalty of a penny a copy — and named for the Maple Leaf Club, a local venue. It went on to sell over a million copies of sheet music, a first for an instrumental piece, and the income it returned changed the conditions of Joplin's life and made his reputation as the "King of Ragtime."

Its significance is larger than its sales. The rag's syncopation — African rhythmic sensibility set against a European march form — became the signature sound of the ragtime era, a music that lent its name to a stretch of American life and laid part of the foundation under jazz. A century of arrangement, transcription, and mechanical reproduction followed. That ubiquity is the whole point of what happened next: a piece duplicated that many times, in that many formats, is exactly the kind of thing that ends up embedded, unasked, in the source of a bridal-shop website.

## Provenance

The piece is public domain, and a hundred years on it is also one of the most duplicated canned MIDIs on the early web. One such file lives embedded in Yvette's Bridal Formal, a Panama City bridal-shop site that pushes its MIDIs at every first visitor. The operator arrived there through a `shouldseethis.com` referral marked, in the entry URL's parameters, "with love" — and was prompted, bluntly, to download the `.mid` on landing. The file was kept, not discarded. That kept file is the seed of the zone's loop.

## The Site, As Found

The shop is real and has traded since 1980. Its website is another matter. Two hundred–odd pages radiate from the homepage like a broom; pink links guide you toward the gowns while side paths loop, dead-end, and circle back. Microsoft Paint art sits beside eighteenth-century portraits. Its categories are intermingled without seam — bridal wear, visual art, poetry, recipes, and a body of "lore." There are pages named for psychological thriller and horror, a portrait painter, and a page called RoadStar1. It does not advertise so much as refuse to stop talking. It is, by wide agreement, one of the strangest sites the web has kept.

## How You Heard It, Then

At publication the average visitor downloaded nothing — the MIDI simply played. It was embedded as background music, and the browsers of that era carried the plumbing to voice it: plugins like QuickTime, the Windows Media Player plugin, and Netscape LiveAudio, sitting over a General MIDI synthesizer the operating system already shipped (on Windows, the Microsoft GS Wavetable Synth). There was no software to choose and no file to open. The page loaded and the room had music in it. A downloaded `.mid` behaved the same way: a double-click handed it to the default media player, and the same built-in synth voiced it. Playing a MIDI was not a specialist act — it was the default, and most people never thought about the machinery under it.

So the intent was ambient autoplay while you browsed. The song was meant to be the room, not a file you fetched — you would land, and it would already be going.

That is also why the experience inverts today. Modern browsers no longer sing MIDI on their own; the plugin era is over and native playback went with it. The same embedded file that once auto-played now has nothing to render it in the page, so the browser falls back to the only thing it can do with an unknown media type — it offers to download it. The operator's blunt prompt to download the `.mid` on first landing is that absence made visible. The song did not change. The browser did.

## Signal Chain

The loop is not a stock recording and not a vague homage. The force-downloaded `.mid` was rendered through Torus — the forthcoming plugin from Circular Ruin, the operator's own audio house — which supplies the "extra-crispy" character, and the output was bounced to the MP3 the zone plays. Stated plainly, the chain is: Yvette's `.mid` → Torus (Circular Ruin) → `mapleleafrag.mp3`.

The two ends of that chain sit in different registers, and the record keeps them apart. What the repository verifies is only that a local MP3 loops behind the gate. That the source was Yvette's MIDI, and that Torus did the shaping, is operator testimony. Both are true; they are simply known in different ways, and a reader is owed the distinction.

## Archive Meaning

A site that will not let you leave without a song is *contre l'oubli* in its rawest, most graceless form. Yvette's hoards attention the way L'Archive hoards evidence — indiscriminately, insistently, past the point of taste. The zone inherits that refusal to be quiet.
